## The tampering boundary (what the guidance did NOT change)

Legal commentary published in July 2026 reads the guidance as clarifying, not loosening, existing law. Points a shop should hold onto:

- **The federal tampering prohibition still applies**, and EPA's 2020 tampering enforcement policy was not withdrawn. That policy has historically aimed enforcement at commercial operators and service shops, not only individual owners — so the "freedom to fix" messaging does not shield the shop.
- **A temporary override is defensible only as a step in a repair.** If an emissions control is bypassed or taken out of certified configuration to diagnose or complete a repair, the vehicle must be back in certified configuration before it leaves the bay. Document the restoration (final scan, monitors, no override left active).
- **"Reasonable basis" protection rests on the shop's paperwork.** Keep the part's certification evidence, the procedure followed, and the before/after readiness record on the RO.
- **OBD-system changes are the highest-risk zone.** Never treat this guidance as cover for disabling, deleting, or reprogramming monitoring.
- **State rules still apply on top.** California's aftermarket-parts approval requirement (CARB Executive Order) and other state/local anti-tampering laws are unaffected.

## Not settled / do not assert

- No statute section, CFR citation, penalty amount, or response deadline should be quoted from this note. Pull exact language from the guidance PDF or get it from counsel.
- Warranty coverage when a non-OEM emissions part is used is **not** guaranteed by the guidance.
- General (non-emissions) repair-data access is still the pending legislative track (REPAIR Act / SAFE Repair Act / H.R. 7389 Motor Vehicle Modernization Act — committee-reported 2026-05-21, no House floor vote found as of 2026-10-13).
